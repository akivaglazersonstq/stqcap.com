A $200 Million Contract Is the Easy Part. Keeping It Is the Game.

A short earning window funding a long life means the portfolio has to do what the career can't — last.

Key takeaways

  1. The 'jock tax' lets multiple states tax athletes on road income by duty days, so choosing a documented residency in a no-income-tax state can outweigh a second endorsement deal.
  2. A short earning window funding a long life means the portfolio must function as a retirement plan starting at 35, where spending rate matters more than contract size.
  3. Trusts, entities, and asset protection are cheap to establish while income is arriving and far costlier to retrofit after the money is already taxed and scattered.

My favorite basketball player just signed a 4-year, $200 million contract. Congratulations to him. Now here's the part nobody puts on SportsCenter: the contract is the easy part. Keeping it is the game.

Before he buys a single thing, there are four moves I'd want made — and none of them require courtside seats or $200 million to matter. They matter for anyone whose money is about to arrive faster than their plan for it.

Solve for taxes before you spend a dollar

He plays in a different state almost every week, and most of them want a cut. The 'jock tax' is real — states tax road income based on duty days spent within their borders. But the bigger lever is where he establishes residency. Choosing a no-income-tax state as a true legal home, documented properly, can be worth more over four years than a second endorsement deal. Tax is the first decision, not the cleanup at the end.

The second move is harder to feel and more important than any of them: separate the career from the money. A short earning window funds a long life. The body might give him eight productive seasons; the portfolio has to fund the next fifty years. The career is the engine. The portfolio is a retirement plan that quietly starts at 35 — which means the spending rate, not the contract size, decides whether it lasts.

Own the boring stuff, build the structure early

Third: own the boring assets. Diversified, tax-aware, liquid. The restaurant his cousin wants to open is not an investment strategy — it's a gift with extra steps, and it's fine to make it as long as nobody pretends it's a portfolio. Most blown fortunes don't die from a bad index fund. They die from concentrated bets, illiquid favors, and the slow leak of saying yes.

Fourth: build the structure now, while the money is arriving — not later, when it's already taxed and scattered. Trusts, entities, and asset protection are cheap to set up and priceless to have. They're far easier to fund as the cash comes in than to retrofit after it's spread across a dozen accounts and a few handshake deals. None of this is exotic. Most advisors just never get around to it before the spending starts.

Who is this wrong for? Nobody, really — but the urgency scales with how fast the money lands and how short the window is. If your income is steady and rising for thirty years, you have time to course-correct. If it shows up in a four-year burst, you don't. You need the plan to matter before the money shows up.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 'jock tax' and how much can it cost?

The jock tax is the practice of states taxing athletes (and other traveling earners) on income earned during work performed within their borders, usually allocated by 'duty days.' For someone playing across many states, it can consume a meaningful share of salary, which is why establishing legal residency in a no-income-tax state is one of the highest-value early decisions.

Do you need to be a pro athlete or have nine figures for this to apply?

No. The principles — solve taxes first, separate career income from long-term money, own diversified liquid assets, and build legal structure early — apply to anyone whose income arrives in a concentrated burst, like a business sale, a windfall, or a high-earning but short career. The dollar figures change; the sequence doesn't.

Why set up trusts and entities before the money arrives instead of after?

Asset protection and tax structures are inexpensive to create and far easier to fund as income flows in, while you still control where it goes. After the money is received, taxed, and spread across accounts and informal deals, retrofitting the same protection is more expensive, more complicated, and sometimes impossible.

Is investing in a friend or family member's business a bad idea?

Not necessarily — but it should be treated as a gift you can afford to lose, not as part of your investment strategy. The mistake is counting illiquid, concentrated, emotionally driven bets as portfolio diversification when they're really favors with financial risk attached.
