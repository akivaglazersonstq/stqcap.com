351 Exchange and the SpaceX IPO: Why It Won't Diversify a Concentrated Position by Itself

A 351 exchange is the move everyone will reach for after the SpaceX IPO. The IRS 25/50 rule means it does not work the way most people think. Here is what a 351 exchange actually does, the tax math, and the real menu for a concentrated position.

AG
Akiva Glazerson
Principal, STQ Capital

Key takeaways

  1. A Section 351 exchange moves an appreciated portfolio into a new ETF with no tax today. Your cost basis and holding period carry over, and the embedded gain is deferred, not erased.
  2. It cannot unwind a single concentrated position on its own. The IRS 25/50 test requires your contribution to already be diversified: no one holding above 25 percent, and your top five below 50 percent.
  3. For a truly concentrated SpaceX-style stake, a 351 is one tool among several. The real answer is usually a sequence: combine holdings to qualify, exchange funds, QSBS, hedging, or charitable gifting.

The SpaceX IPO is coming, and a wave of newly liquid shareholders is about to go looking for a way to diversify without a giant tax bill.

The strategy they will hear about most is the Section 351 exchange. It came up at nearly every session of the Basis Northwest tax conference this year.

It is a genuinely powerful tool. It is also widely misunderstood. Used the way most people describe it, on a single concentrated position, it does not work.

What a 351 exchange actually does

A Section 351 exchange, named for Section 351 of the Internal Revenue Code, lets a group of investors contribute securities into a newly launched exchange-traded fund (ETF) in exchange for shares of that fund, with no tax due at the time of the transfer.

Your cost basis, what you originally paid, and your holding period come along for the ride. The embedded gain is not realized. It is deferred inside your new ETF shares until you sell them.

The appeal is obvious. You go from an old, tax-locked portfolio to a clean, diversified ETF, and you do not write a check to the IRS to get there.

The gain does not disappear. It moves into your ETF shares and waits.

The catch nobody mentions: the 25/50 diversification test

Here is the part that gets lost in the LinkedIn version of this idea. The IRS will not let you drop a single concentrated stock into a 351 ETF to escape the concentration, and the reason is written into the statute.

Section 351(e) of the code switches off the tax-free treatment when you transfer property to what it calls an investment company, which is exactly what a lone concentrated position would create. To avoid that label, your contribution has to be diversified under the standard borrowed from Section 368(a)(2)(F): no single holding above 25 percent of what you contribute, and your five largest holdings together no more than 50 percent. Practitioners shorthand it as the 25/50 test. (Sources: IRC 351(e), Kitces, Plancorp.)

In plain terms: your portfolio has to be reasonably diversified before you contribute it. So a 351 exchange is excellent for repositioning a sprawling, appreciated, tax-locked portfolio. It is not, by itself, a way to diversify out of one giant SpaceX position.

A worked example, where it does fit

Consider an investor with $3,000,000 spread across roughly twenty long-held names. The largest sits at 22 percent, the top five at 46 percent, so the portfolio passes the 25/50 test. Cost basis is $1,000,000, leaving a $2,000,000 embedded gain.

Selling to rebalance would realize that $2,000,000 as a long-term gain. At the top 2026 federal rate of 20 percent plus the 3.8 percent net investment income tax, that is $476,000 of federal tax. Add California at its top 13.3 percent rate and you are near $742,000 due this year. (Illustrative, top brackets, 2026.)

Through a 351 exchange, the same investor contributes the portfolio into a launching ETF, owes nothing this year, and carries the $2,000,000 gain forward in the new shares. Same diversification goal. The tax bill is deferred, not paid.

What about a truly concentrated SpaceX position?

This is the real question, and the honest answer is that a 351 alone will not solve it. A position that is 80 or 90 percent of your net worth fails the 25 percent limit on its own.

The realistic playbook is a sequence, not a single move:

  • Pair the position with enough other securities so that no holding exceeds 25 percent, then contribute the combined, qualifying portfolio.
  • Use an exchange fund, a different structure that accepts concentrated stock but typically locks your capital up for about seven years and is limited to qualified purchasers.
  • Check whether the shares are qualified small business stock (QSBS) under Section 1202, which can exclude a large share of the gain outright. The catch: Section 1202 requires the company to have had $50 million or less in gross assets when the stock was issued, so for a company the size of SpaceX only the earliest investors and employees are likely to qualify.
  • Hedge and borrow against the position to create liquidity now, and stage the diversification over several tax years.
  • Give a slice to a donor-advised fund (DAF), removing the gain entirely on the donated shares while funding your charitable goals.

A 351 exchange can be the final step in that sequence, once the position has been brought into a qualifying, diversified pool. It is rarely the first.

Where this does not apply

A 351 exchange is the wrong tool if your portfolio cannot meet the 25/50 test and cannot be combined to get there, if you need the cash rather than diversification, or if you expect to hold the position until death, when a step-up in basis may erase the gain anyway.

It also requires timing. You generally have to contribute while a sponsor is seeding a new fund, not on any random Tuesday. A handful of ETF sponsors now run these conversions and publish education on the mechanics. (See Alpha Architect's 351 Education Center.)

How STQ thinks about it

For a client heading into the SpaceX IPO, we do not start with the structure. We start with the position: how concentrated it is, the cost basis, the holding period, whether it qualifies as QSBS, and when the lockup ends.

Then we sequence the tools. Sometimes a 351 exchange is the elegant finish. Often it is one piece of a longer plan that begins well before the shares are liquid.

The worst outcome is the common one: waiting until the stock is sellable, then selling in a panic and handing a quarter of the gain to two tax authorities that were never going to chase you for it.

Plan early. Diversify deliberately. Pay the tax on your schedule, not the market's.

Frequently asked questions

Can a 351 exchange diversify a single concentrated stock like SpaceX?

Not on its own. The IRS 25/50 test requires that no holding be more than 25 percent of your contribution, so a single dominant position does not qualify. It has to be combined with enough other securities to pass the test first, or paired with another structure such as an exchange fund.

What is the 25/50 diversification test?

No single security may exceed 25 percent of the portfolio you contribute, and your five largest holdings together may not exceed 50 percent. ETFs are looked through to their underlying holdings when applying the test.

Do I ever pay the tax?

Yes, when you sell your ETF shares. The deferred gain rides along on your original cost basis. If you hold until death, your heirs may receive a step-up in basis that can erase the deferred gain under current law.

How is a 351 ETF exchange different from a traditional exchange fund?

An exchange fund is a partnership that can accept a concentrated position but typically locks your money up for about seven years and is limited to qualified purchasers. A 351 conversion gives you liquid ETF shares with no seven-year lock, but your contribution must pass the 25/50 test.

Does the SpaceX IPO timing matter?

Yes. A 351 contribution generally has to happen while a sponsor is seeding a new ETF, and you usually cannot transfer shares until the post-IPO lockup expires. Both argue for planning before the shares are liquid.

Who actually runs 351 conversions?

A handful of ETF sponsors, including firms such as Alpha Architect and Cambria, now offer them and publish education on the mechanics. The structuring and the diversification testing are best handled with a tax advisor.
