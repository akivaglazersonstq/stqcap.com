Executive Summary

A concentrated, low-basis position from an initial public offering (IPO) leaves most people with a bad menu: hold it and stay fully exposed to one stock, or sell it and realize the gain.

At the top 2026 federal rate of 20 percent long-term capital gains plus the 3.8 percent net investment income tax (NIIT), that is 23.8 percent before any state tax (IRS, 2026). The point of this playbook is the third path between those two.

There is no single move. There are five, and they reinforce each other. A Section 351 exchange (26 U.S. Code Section 351) diversifies the bulk of the position with no tax today. Direct indexing rebuilds broad-market exposure as a tax engine. Systematic loss harvesting banks losses to offset gains. Box spread financing raises cash near Treasury rates without selling. A synthetic variable prepaid forward protects and monetizes whatever concentrated stake remains.

Most of these strategies defer tax rather than erase it. The deferred gain rides inside your new shares, and if you hold until death, your heirs may receive a step-up in basis that resets cost basis to fair market value and can wipe out the embedded gain entirely under current law.

Timing matters more than people expect. A 351 contribution generally has to happen while a sponsor is seeding a new exchange-traded fund (ETF), and you usually cannot transfer shares until the post-IPO lockup expires. Both argue for planning before the shares are liquid, not after.

Every structure here is built from instruments you control and can be priced against the market, not bought as an opaque, marked-up bank product. Most advisors have never built them. We build them regularly.

These are tools for a specific profile: a holder of a large taxable position with meaningful embedded gain who wants to diversify, finance, or protect without a forced sale. They are wrong for someone who simply wants out of the stock, who holds only in tax-advantaged accounts, or who lacks the liquid collateral the financing legs require.

The five moves at a glance

  1. Section 351 exchange. Roll the bulk of the position into a diversified ETF with no tax today. The gain carries forward, deferred.
  2. Direct indexing. Rebuild broad-market exposure as individual stocks so the portfolio harvests losses continuously underneath.
  3. Long-short tax-loss harvesting. Bank losses systematically and wash-sale-clean, building an inventory to offset the eventual gain.
  4. Box spread financing. Raise cash near Treasury rates without selling, with the cost treated as a Section 1256 capital loss.
  5. Synthetic variable prepaid forward. Protect and monetize the concentrated stake you keep, spaced clear of the constructive sale rule.

The Sequence

The five moves are not a menu you pick one item from. They are an ordered system, and knowing which move answers which need is most of the work.

01

The Section 351 Exchange

The problem it solves

You hold a large, low-basis position and want to diversify, but selling to rebalance realizes the entire embedded gain and hands a fifth or more of it to the IRS before you can reinvest a dollar.

How it works

A Section 351 exchange, named for Section 351 of the Internal Revenue Code (26 U.S. Code Section 351), lets a group of investors contribute securities into a newly launched exchange-traded fund (ETF, a pooled, listed fund) in exchange for shares of that fund, with no tax due at the time of the transfer. Your cost basis (what you originally paid) and your holding period carry over. The embedded gain is not realized. It is deferred inside your new ETF shares until you sell them.

You go from an old, tax-locked single position to a clean, diversified, liquid fund without writing a check to the IRS to get there. You pay the tax only when you sell the ETF shares, on your schedule rather than the market's, and if you hold until death the step-up in basis may erase the deferred gain entirely under current law.

This is a deferral, not an erasure, and it differs from a traditional exchange fund, which is a partnership that typically locks money up for about seven years and is limited to qualified purchasers. A 351 conversion gives you liquid ETF shares with no seven-year lock.

Illustrative worked example, top brackets, 2026

Consider an investor with $3,000,000 spread across long-held names, a $1,000,000 cost basis, and a $2,000,000 embedded long-term gain. Selling to rebalance realizes that $2,000,000 gain. At the top 2026 federal rate of 20 percent plus the 3.8 percent NIIT, that is $476,000 of federal tax, and adding California at its top 13.3 percent rate brings the bill to roughly $742,000 due this year.

Through a 351 exchange, the same investor contributes the portfolio into a launching ETF, owes nothing this year, and carries the $2,000,000 gain forward inside the new diversified shares. Same diversification goal, tax deferred rather than paid.

Right for

An investor carrying a large, appreciated, concentrated or low-basis portfolio who wants to diversify and is willing to plan ahead of a fund launch and ahead of the lockup expiring.

Wrong for

Someone who needs cash today rather than diversification, or who expects to hold the position until death when a step-up in basis may wipe out the gain anyway. Other paths may serve them better.

Direct Indexing

The problem it solves

After you diversify, you still want broad-market exposure, but a plain index fund gives you the return and nothing else. When one stock inside the index falls, that loss is trapped inside the fund and you cannot use it against your other gains, and the S&P 500 itself is far more concentrated than it looks.

How it works

Direct indexing means owning the individual securities that make up an index, all of them or a representative sample, rather than a fund that holds them for you. The market exposure is essentially identical to an S&P 500 ETF. The tax treatment is completely different.

Because you own the individual stocks, you can harvest losses at the security level: when one name drops while the broader index is flat, you sell it, immediately buy a similar stock to keep your exposure, and bank the loss as a tax asset. Individual stocks move independently, so even in a rising market some names are down, which means harvesting opportunities appear continuously.

Those banked losses can be deployed against gains anywhere in your portfolio: a business exit, a real estate sale, a restricted stock unit (RSU) vest, or reducing the concentrated position itself. This also addresses a hidden problem with the index: market-cap weighting has pushed the top 10 S&P 500 companies from 19 percent of the index in 1990 to more than 40 percent today (as of 2026), with those same 10 names capturing about 34 percent of all S&P 500 profits (Apollo Chief Economist, 2026). Direct indexing lets you keep the foundation while building around it intentionally.

Long-Short Tax-Loss Harvesting

The problem it solves

A directly indexed portfolio in a calm, rising market only throws off so many losses, yet you have a very large embedded gain to offset. You need a bigger, more reliable inventory of harvestable losses, and you need to capture them without tripping the wash sale rule.

How it works

Tax-loss harvesting means deliberately selling a position that has fallen below its cost basis, locking in the loss on paper, and immediately reinvesting in something similar so your market exposure stays essentially the same. You have not changed your strategy or permanently exited; you have converted a paper loss into a tax asset, what we call a loss bank.

The one rule to respect is the IRS wash sale rule: if you buy the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale, the loss is disallowed, so you buy something similar but not identical (for example, sell one broad index fund and buy a total-market fund).

The goal is to systematically build an inventory of losses that sits in the portfolio until a taxable event arrives (a business sale, a real estate gain, equity compensation, or reducing the concentrated stake), at which point the losses are already there to offset it. In this framework volatility is not a problem to manage, it is the raw material: the more the market moves, the more harvest opportunities appear and the larger the loss bank grows.

Paired with box spread financing, the combination compounds, because the harvested losses offset gains while the box spread loan can generate deductible interest, with that interest treated as a capital loss under IRS Section 1256 (60 percent long-term, 40 percent short-term).

Illustrative worked example

An investor expects a large gain event ahead, for example reducing the concentrated stake or a business sale. By harvesting losses systematically through the years beforehand, the portfolio builds a loss bank that offsets that gain dollar-for-dollar when it lands, sheltering income that would otherwise be taxed at up to 23.8 percent federal (IRS, 2026) before state tax. The earlier the harvesting starts, the larger the offset waiting when the event arrives.

Going further: the long-short (130-30) tax-aware extension

A long-only directly indexed portfolio can only harvest losses on the names it actually owns, and in a steadily rising market the supply of losers shrinks. A long-short overlay is the institutional answer to that ceiling. In a tax-aware long-short structure, often run as a "130-30" portfolio, the manager holds roughly 130 percent of capital in long positions and sells short roughly 30 percent, financed so the net market exposure stays close to 100 percent.

Short selling means borrowing a stock and selling it, intending to buy it back later, so a short position gains value when the stock falls. The point for tax purposes is mechanical: in any market, some longs fall and some shorts rise the wrong way, so both books continuously throw off realizable losses. That widens the inventory of harvestable losses well beyond what a long-only portfolio can produce, which is exactly what a holder with a very large embedded gain needs.

The trade-offs are real. A long-short overlay adds leverage, short-borrow costs, and greater complexity, and short-sale tax rules differ from long-sale rules (for example, gains on shorts are generally short-term regardless of holding period). It suits larger taxable portfolios that need more loss capacity than long-only harvesting can supply, and it is not appropriate for everyone.

Sourcing and scope note

This long-short (130-30) extension is described from general industry and academic practice on tax-aware long-short investing as of June 2026 (see, for example, published research from quantitative asset managers such as AQR and Quantinno, and the broader tax-aware long-short literature). It goes beyond STQ Capital's existing published insights and is included here for education. It is not a description of a specific STQ product or a claim about STQ-managed results. Box Spread Financing

The problem it solves

You need liquidity, for a home, a tax bill, a private deal, or to reinvest, but you do not want to sell appreciated positions and trigger the gain. The usual answer is a bank line of credit at whatever rate the bank feels like charging, with an application, a credit check, and terms it can change on you.

How it works

A box spread is a package of S&P 500 index options (SPX, or its one-tenth-size sibling XSP) that, combined, behaves like a fixed-rate loan. It is a four-leg structure (a bull call spread plus a bear put spread at the same strikes, same index, same expiration) deliberately built so the payoff at expiration is fixed no matter where the market goes.

Because the payoff is known, you can calculate the implied interest rate before you trade: the difference between the cash you receive today and the fixed amount you owe at expiration. That rate is set by the options market in real time, with institutions competing on both sides, and it has historically priced close to Treasury yields, well below most margin accounts, home equity lines of credit (HELOCs), or bank loans.

You sell the box spread, cash lands in your account, your portfolio stays fully invested with no positions sold and no gain triggered, and at expiration you settle the fixed amount or roll it forward. The strategy works only with European-style index options (SPX or XSP), where early exercise is impossible, which is what guarantees the fixed payoff.

The interest may be deductible as investment interest expense, with the deduction following the loan rather than its use, and box spread interest is treated as a capital loss under IRS Section 1256 (60 percent long-term, 40 percent short-term). Because you never sell, appreciated positions keep compounding and the step-up in basis at death stays intact.

The Synthetic Variable Prepaid Forward

The problem it solves

After diversifying the bulk, you keep a concentrated stake you still believe in but cannot easily sell. You want downside protection and cash today without the taxable sale, and without the markup and lock-in of a packaged bank product.

How it works

A variable prepaid forward (VPF) is a contract in which you receive cash today in return for agreeing to deliver a variable number of shares at a future date, with a band of price protection built in. Wall Street has sold packaged VPFs to concentrated holders for decades, but the economics are not magic and can be rebuilt transparently from listed instruments you control.

First, the collar: with the stock at, say, 100, you buy a put option at 90 (a put gives you the right to sell at the strike, so it sets a floor below which your downside is capped) and you sell a call option at 110 (a call obligates you to sell at the strike if exercised, capping your upside above 110, and the premium you collect helps pay for the put). The long put and short call together bracket the position: protected below 90, capped above 110, free to move in between.

Second, the box spread provides the cash: a package of S&P 500 index options (SPX) that behaves like a fixed-rate loan priced near Treasury yields. Layer the box spread on the collared position and you have rebuilt a VPF from parts you fully control. No shares are sold, so there is no realized gain and no check to the IRS at execution; the gain stays deferred and the step-up in basis at death is preserved.

The one thing to plan around is the constructive sale rule under Section 1259 of the Internal Revenue Code: a hedge that strips out nearly all of your risk and reward can be treated as if you sold the stock, so the put and call strikes must be spaced widely enough to retain meaningful exposure. That spacing is a deliberate planning decision.

Illustrative worked example, top brackets, 2026

An investor holds $3,000,000 of a single appreciated stock with a $1,000,000 cost basis and a $2,000,000 embedded long-term gain. Selling to de-risk realizes that gain and costs $476,000 of federal tax this year at the top rate of 20 percent plus the 3.8 percent NIIT, before state tax, and surrenders the future upside.

Through a synthetic VPF, the same investor instead collars the position (illustrative strikes: put at 90, call at 110) and raises cash with a box spread. No shares are sold, so there is no gain to report and no check to the IRS this year. The downside is floored, a defined slice of upside is retained, the borrowed cash is available to diversify or spend, and the gain stays deferred inside shares the investor still owns, with the potential step-up in basis at death intact.

Right for

A holder of a large, low-basis, concentrated position who wants downside protection and liquidity while staying invested, and who has the liquid collateral the box spread requires.

Wrong for

Someone willing to give up all upside, who lacks liquid collateral, who simply wants out of the stock entirely (a clean sale may be better), or who expects to hold until a step-up wipes out the gain anyway.

A Full Walk-Through

Meet Maya, a hypothetical, illustrative holder. The point of walking one person through all five moves in order is to show how they compound, not to recommend these exact figures for anyone.

Maya was an early employee at NewCo, which just went public. When the lockup lifts she holds $10,000,000 of NewCo stock against a $1,000,000 cost basis, leaving a $9,000,000 embedded long-term gain. If she simply sold to de-risk, the gain would cost $2,142,000 in federal tax at the top 2026 rate of 20 percent long-term capital gains plus the 3.8 percent NIIT (23.8 percent), before any California tax. She does not want to write that check, and she does not want to stay 100 percent exposed to one stock. Here is the sequence she runs instead.

Move 1: diversify the bulk with a Section 351 exchange

Maya contributes $8,000,000 (the bulk) into a launching diversified ETF. No shares are sold, so there is no tax this year. The roughly $7,200,000 of embedded gain on that slice carries forward inside her new fund shares. She keeps the remaining $2,000,000 of NewCo, the stake she still believes in. Tax due on this move: zero. Selling that slice instead would have cost about $1,713,600 federal.

Move 2: rebuild exposure through direct indexing

Rather than hold that $8,000,000 as a plain index fund, Maya holds it as a directly indexed S&P 500 sleeve: the same broad-market exposure, but owning the individual names so losses can be harvested underneath. At the published 1 percent to 2 percent annual tax-alpha range, that is roughly $80,000 to $160,000 of harvested losses banked per year, without changing her market exposure.

Move 3: amplify the losses with long-short harvesting

Maya knows a gain event is coming (she intends to trim the concentrated NewCo stake over time, and expects other gains). A tax-aware long-short overlay widens the supply of harvestable losses well beyond what the long-only sleeve produces. Run systematically, the loss bank grows to an illustrative $700,000 of net realized losses over about five years, sitting ready to offset gains dollar-for-dollar. At 23.8 percent federal, that is roughly $166,600 of tax sheltered when she finally realizes those gains.

Move 4: finance liquidity with a box spread

Maya needs $2,000,000 in cash for a home and a private deal. Instead of selling (which would realize gain), she raises it with a one-year box spread at an implied rate near 4.25 percent, about $85,000 of interest, treated as a Section 1256 capital loss (60 percent long-term, 40 percent short-term). Her portfolio stays fully invested and nothing is sold. Tax triggered: zero.

Move 5: protect the rest with a synthetic VPF

On the $2,000,000 of NewCo she kept, Maya builds a synthetic variable prepaid forward: a collar (illustrative strikes, put at 90 and call at 110) for downside protection, plus a box spread for additional cash. No shares are sold, the gain stays deferred, the downside is floored, a defined slice of upside is retained, and the step-up in basis at death stays intact. The strikes are spaced wide enough to stay clear of the Section 1259 constructive sale rule.

How These Fit Together

Sequence the five moves rather than picking one. Plan before the lockup ends, because the best options expire with it.

Step one, diversify the bulk: use a Section 351 exchange to roll most of the concentrated position into a diversified ETF with no tax today, carrying the embedded gain forward instead of paying it.

Step two, rebuild exposure intelligently: hold that broad-market exposure through direct indexing rather than a plain fund, so the portfolio quietly harvests losses at the individual-stock level.

Step three, amplify the losses: run systematic, wash-sale-clean tax-loss harvesting to build a loss bank that will offset gains when a taxable event eventually lands.

Step four, finance liquidity without selling: when you need cash, raise it with a box spread at near-Treasury rates so appreciated positions keep compounding and the step-up stays intact, with the interest treated as a capital loss under IRS Section 1256.

Step five, protect and monetize the rest: for the concentrated stake you keep, build a synthetic variable prepaid forward, a collar for downside protection plus a box spread for cash, spaced to stay clear of the Section 1259 constructive sale rule.

Each move makes the next more powerful: the 351 diversifies, direct indexing and harvesting build the offset, the box spread funds liquidity and feeds deductible interest into the same return, and the synthetic VPF guards what remains. Held to the end, the step-up in basis can reset cost basis at death and erase the deferred gain entirely under current law.

Stay invested, defer deliberately, and pay the tax on your schedule, not the market's.
